Finding a co-op RPG that respects your calendar is harder than it sounds, and that is exactly the problem this Wyldheart review sets out to examine. Wayfinder Studios' debut blends tabletop-style adventuring with survival-game combat, then wraps both around campaigns short enough that a group of friends can actually finish one. A Wyldheart review that ignored group logistics would miss the point, because scheduling — not difficulty — is what kills most multiplayer campaigns.

Wyldheart is the first title from Wayfinder Studios, a team founded by veterans with credits at EA DICE, Embark Studios, and Mojang Studios, including work on titles such as *Star Wars Battlefront II*. Creative Director Dennis Brännvall and Marketing Director Erin Bower walked press through an early build ahead of the game's full reveal, and the framing stayed consistent: shorter adventures, smaller communities, and a genuine sense of completion.

## What Is Wyldheart?

Wyldheart is a cooperative action RPG built around discrete, self-contained campaigns rather than one endless character save. Each campaign is planned to run roughly ten hours for its main story, with around fifteen hours of optional content for groups that want to poke at every corner of the map.

That structure is the whole pitch. Instead of asking four adults to protect the same Tuesday night for six months, Wyldheart asks them to commit to a single adventure. Finish it, and you can start a fresh campaign with the same group — or carry a character into a run with entirely different friends.

| Feature | What the early build showed |
|---|---|
| Studio | Wayfinder Studios (debut title) |
| Campaign length | ~10 hours main story, ~15 hours optional content |
| Party size | Up to 4 players |
| Multiplayer style | Drop-in / drop-out |
| Saves | Cloud-shared across the group |
| Progression | Classless, with group XP |
| Difficulty | Scales with the number of players |
| World map | Hexagonal grid inspired by board games |
| Catch-up systems | Built in, so missed sessions don't stall a group |

One caveat worth repeating: these figures come from a pre-release build shown to press, so numbers and features may shift before launch. Community reports are still thin simply because the game has only just been revealed.

## Character Creation and a Classless Progression System

Every campaign opens with character creation. You choose an ancestry, a background, and a set of appearance options, which gives you both a mechanical starting point and some narrative flavor before the first dungeon even loads.

### Why "Classless" Matters More Than It Sounds

Wyldheart does not lock you into a class. Your background suggests a direction — a particular upbringing might nudge you toward support tools or heavier weapons — but nothing stops you from drifting somewhere else entirely. For players who hate rerolling because they picked the "wrong" archetype at level one, that flexibility is a meaningful quality-of-life win.

| Your choice | What it shapes | Are you locked in? |
|---|---|---|
| Ancestry | Character identity and flavor | No |
| Background | Suggested starting direction | No — follow it or diverge |
| Appearance | Cosmetic look | N/A |
| Weapon loadout | 2 of your 5 quickslots | No — swap freely |
| Utility items | The other 3 quickslots | No — swap freely |

The trade-off is choice paralysis. Without a class to anchor you, your first hour can feel open-ended, and the early build offered little hand-holding. If you are the type of player who wants a clear "this is your role" label, plan to spend a few minutes reading tooltips before your group dives in.

## The Hope Mechanic Explained

If there is one system that defines a Wyldheart review, it is hope. Each character begins with five hearts displayed above the health bar, and those hearts act as a buffer between a bad night and a dead hero.

Getting knocked out costs a heart. Traveling through the middle of the night costs a heart too, which turns route planning into a real decision rather than a formality. Towns restore lost hearts, so a wise party treats a settlement visit as maintenance, not just shopping.

| Hearts remaining | State | What it means in play |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Full hope | Normal adventuring, no penalty |
| 1–4 | Reduced hope | A warning sign; detour to town and recover |
| 0 | Hopeless | The next knockout kills the character |
| Dead | Defeated | Revive at a temple or with a revive scroll |

Death is not necessarily permanent, and this is where the tabletop influence shows. Friends can haul a fallen character to a temple or burn a revive scroll to bring them back. Alternatively, the player can roll a brand-new character, rejoin the party, and eventually use that newcomer to rescue their own original hero. It is a surprisingly elegant answer to the "my character died and now I sit out for two hours" problem that plagues so many co-op campaigns.

## Multiplayer, Dungeons, and Loot

Multiplayer is clearly designed around real-life scheduling chaos. Up to four players can drop in and out, saves are cloud-shared, XP is pooled across the group, difficulty scales with party size, and catch-up mechanics keep anyone who missed a session from falling permanently behind.

| Multiplayer feature | Why it matters in practice |
|---|---|
| Drop-in / drop-out | No one has to commit to every session |
| Cloud-shared saves | Any player can host without losing progress |
| Group XP | No one gets punished for playing a support role |
| Scaling difficulty | Two players and four players both get a fair fight |
| Catch-up mechanics | A missed week doesn't mean a useless character |
| Multiplayer dialogue voting | The party decides story responses together |

### Dungeons Without Minimaps

Wyldheart deliberately strips minimaps out of its dungeons to keep players immersed in the space. Instead, you are encouraged to drop way markers, or to light torches and use them as landmarks for rooms you have already cleared. The early slime dungeon even hid a hole you could jump into, with a rope providing the way back out.

That design choice cuts both ways. It creates genuine exploration and memorable "wait, which corridor was that?" moments, but groups with a poor sense of direction should expect to spend extra time wandering.

### Loot, Durability, and Improvised Weapons

The build was generous with loot, scattering weapons and items through the environment and dropping more from enemies. Durability is a factor: weapons break after enough use, so carrying spares is not optional.

| Weapon category | Seen in the early build | Practical note |
|---|---|---|
| Swords | Yes | Reliable close-range standby |
| Axes and maces | Yes | Heavier melee options |
| Bows | Yes | Ranged option for pulling enemies |
| Shields | Yes | Defensive slot for blocking |
| Improvised | Yes (including a large thighbone) | Signals a playful loot table |

The dungeon's climax was a giant slime boss that rewarded environmental awareness. Its weakness to fire meant that grabbing the braziers placed around the arena and throwing them dealt damage far faster than trading hits head-on.

## World Map, Camping, and Downtime

Once you escape the opening dungeon, the adventure opens onto a hexagonal world map clearly inspired by board games. Every hex can be explored, and beyond towns and dungeons you will find a spread of handcrafted events and scenes rather than procedurally generated filler.

Camping gives the map its downtime rhythm. When you make camp you can cook, craft, repair worn gear, and rest — a loop that turns a long journey into a series of small, manageable stops. For groups juggling limited play sessions, that natural pause point is a quiet advantage.

## Verdict: Is Wyldheart Worth Following?

Based on the early build, Wyldheart is one of the more thoughtfully positioned co-op RPGs on the horizon. It is not trying to out-scale the biggest survival games; it is trying to be finishable. That is a refreshing goal, and the hope mechanic gives it a genuine identity beyond "survival game with swords."

| Strength | Consideration |
|---|---|
| Ten-hour campaigns you can actually complete | May feel short to players who want a 100-hour epic |
| Drop-in co-op with catch-up mechanics | Still requires some coordination to feel seamless |
| Classless builds and flexible loadouts | Choice paralysis without clear guidance |
| Hope mechanic adds real stakes | Punishing for reckless or directionally challenged groups |
| Hex map with handcrafted events | No minimap in dungeons will frustrate some players |

Two honest caveats. The build shown to press lacked much of its narrative and presentational polish, including voice acting, so the story's quality remains unproven. And because the game had only just been revealed, player experience is limited — there is not yet a large community consensus to lean on. ## FAQ

**How long does a Wyldheart campaign take to finish?**
Wayfinder Studios is targeting roughly ten hours for a campaign's main story, with about fifteen hours of optional side content on top of that. Groups that play in short weekly sessions should be able to wrap an adventure in a handful of sittings rather than months.

**How many players can join a Wyldheart campaign?**
Up to four players can adventure together, with drop-in/drop-out support, cloud-shared saves, and group XP. Difficulty scales with the number of players, so a two-person run and a four-person run both stay playable.

**What happens when your character dies in Wyldheart?**
Running out of hope hearts and getting knocked out again kills the character. Friends can revive them at a temple or with a revive scroll, or the player can create a new character and eventually use that new hero to rescue the original one.

**Is Wyldheart a survival game or a tabletop RPG?**
It borrows from both. The loop of scavenging loot, managing weapon durability, and camping to cook and repair comes from co-op survival games, while ancestry, background, hope hearts, and dice-free campaign arcs pull directly from tabletop roleplaying.
